01 · About

Two questions I keep coming back to

The first is why harm from drugs lands so unevenly on some neighborhoods and not others. The second is what a high school student can actually do about it. For the last two years I have worked on both at once: a research project that measures what happens around supervised consumption sites in New York City, an app and library workshop that teach younger students how addiction works before anyone has to learn it the hard way, and an internship with a county prevention agency.

I grew up in Fort Lee, in a Korean-American family, and most of what I do outside of class involves other people: teaching Korean to young children and to non-Korean adults at my church, tutoring elementary students in math and English, and helping run events for the borough’s Youth Council. I also paint and write, and some of that work has found its way into galleries and the Scholastic Awards.

I plan to study the health sciences, with the same goal that runs through my research and my app: reaching people before a crisis does.

“Education is the best form of prevention.” That line is in the first screen of my app, and it is the shortest version of what I believe.

02 · Research

Beyond the facility walls

In 2021 New York City opened the first supervised consumption sites in the United States, in East Harlem and Washington Heights. The debate about them is usually about what happens inside: overdoses reversed, referrals made. I wanted to know what happens outside, on the blocks around them, because that is where neighbors form their opinions and where the political fight is actually won or lost.

Mentored since July 2025 by Brian Hyunsoo Song, a biostatistician at Johns Hopkins University, my co-author and I used two public signals as proxies for visible public drug use: 311 syringe complaints and EMS drug-related calls. We compared the months before and after the sites opened, close to the sites and farther away.

  • Methods: Moran’s I spatial clustering, a difference-in-differences comparison, and logistic regression, all in R
  • Finding: zip codes close to the sites saw about a 3 percent larger reduction in EMS drug calls and a 35 percent larger reduction in syringe complaints than zip codes farther away
  • I wrote the literature review, the study rationale, and the abstract, and worked with my co-author on the analysis and on the revision the reviewers accepted
  • Manuscript accepted for publication in NHSJS Reports after peer review (2026); presented at the Terra North Jersey STEM Fair, March 21 to 22, 2026

03 · SafeSteps For All

An app for the conversation nobody wants to have

Most drug education I had seen was either a scare poster or a pamphlet. Neither explains the part that actually matters to a young student: how tolerance builds, why withdrawal feels the way it does, and how a pill from a friend can turn out to be fentanyl. In January 2026 I built SafeSteps For All, an evidence-based drug-education app, and published it on the Apple App Store. I had no coding background, so I taught myself from video tutorials, tried platform after platform, and worked through the App Store upload problems by asking questions in developer forums and Discord servers.

The app walks through drug categories, street names, short- and long-term effects, and the mechanics of addiction, with quizzes so the information sticks. I did not want it to stay on a screen, so I turned it into a 45-minute interactive workshop for grades 4 to 7 and took it to public libraries, including Leonia Public Library and Palisades Park Public Library in June 2026. More than 70 students have taken part so far.
