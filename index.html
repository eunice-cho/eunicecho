01 · About

Two questions I keep coming back to

The first is why harm from drugs lands so unevenly on some neighborhoods and not others. The second is what a high school student can actually do about it. For the last two years I have worked on both at once: a research project that measures what happens around supervised consumption sites in New York City, and an app that teaches teenagers how addiction works before anyone has to learn it the hard way.

I grew up in Fort Lee, in a Korean-American family, and most of what I do outside of class involves other people’s kids: teaching Korean to four-year-olds on weekend mornings, tutoring elementary students in math and reading, and running events for the borough’s Youth Council. I also paint and write, and some of that work has found its way into galleries and the Scholastic Awards.

I plan to study public health, with epidemiology as the method and community health education as the point.

“Education is the best form of prevention.” That line is in the first screen of my app, and it is the shortest version of what I believe.

02 · Research

Beyond the facility walls

In 2021 New York City opened the first supervised consumption sites in the United States. The debate about them is usually about what happens inside: overdoses reversed, referrals made. I wanted to know what happens outside, on the blocks around them, because that is where neighbors form their opinions and where the political fight is actually won or lost.

Working under a faculty biostatistician at Johns Hopkins University as a research intern since July 2025, my co-author and I used two public signals as proxies for visible public drug use: 311 syringe complaints and EMS drug-related calls. We compared the months before and after the sites opened, close to the sites and farther away.

  • Methods: Moran’s I spatial clustering, a difference-in-differences comparison, and logistic regression, all in R
  • Finding: zip codes close to the sites saw about a 3 percent larger reduction in EMS drug calls and a 35 percent larger reduction in syringe complaints than zip codes farther away
  • I wrote the literature review, the study rationale, and the abstract, and worked with my co-author on the analysis and on the revision the reviewers accepted
  • Manuscript accepted for publication in NHSJS Reports after peer review (2026); presented at the Terra North Jersey STEM Fair, March 21 to 22, 2026

A second project, with a team for the Toshiba/NSTA ExploraVision competition, proposed a non-invasive screening tool for liver cirrhosis and earned an Honorable Mention in 2026.

03 · SafeSteps For All

An app for the conversation nobody wants to have

Most drug education I had seen was either a scare poster or a pamphlet. Neither explains the part that actually matters to a teenager: how tolerance builds, why withdrawal feels the way it does, and how a pill from a friend can turn into fentanyl. So in January 2026 I built SafeSteps For All, an evidence-based drug-education app, and published it on the Apple App Store.

The app walks through drug categories, street names, short- and long-term effects, and the mechanics of addiction, with quizzes so the information sticks. In April 2026 I launched a companion workshop at the public library that teaches students to weigh consequences the way the app does.

  • Presented the program to community audiences with The Center for Alcohol and Drug Resources (TCADR), a county prevention agency in Fair Lawn
  • Designing the program for TCADR’s 2026 Seeds of Change Youth Conference at Ramapo College, October 23, 2026
